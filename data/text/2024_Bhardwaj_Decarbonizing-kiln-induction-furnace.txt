Decarbonizing rotary kiln–induction furnace based sponge iron production
Nishant Bhardwaj , Srinivas Seethamraju , Santanu Bandyopadhyay *
Department of Energy Science and Engineering, Indian Institute of Technology Bombay, Powai, Mumbai, 400076, India

A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Handling editor: Isabel Soares

The direct carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions from the iron and steel sector are nearly 7 % of the global CO2
emissions from energy use. India is the world’s second-largest producer of steel and the largest sponge iron
producer. India produces one-third of the global sponge iron, mostly from rotary kilns using coal as an energy
source, resulting in higher energy and emission intensities than the global average. This study evaluates major
options for CO2 abatement in the rotary kiln–induction furnace process for steel production. Based on the mass
and energy balances of a typical sponge iron plant, this study evaluates the techno-economic potential of nine
major decarbonization measures. The proposed decarbonization measures include improved energy efficiency,
improved material efficiency, renewable energy use, and fuel substitution. Marginal abatement and green premium curves are generated by incorporating the dependency of different measures. Depending upon the set of
options chosen, carbon abatement is in the range of 55.8 %–99.2 %. It is observed that 11.5 %–20.6 % of
decarbonization potential can be achieved without any additional cost to the customer. These findings are
insightful for policymakers and industry stakeholders and provide a foundation for designing and implementing
a net-zero pathway for the sector.

1. Introduction
Greenhouse gases increase the temperature of the atmosphere,
eventually resulting in global warming. Carbon dioxide (CO2), methane,
nitrous oxide, and fluorinated gases are the prominent greenhouse
gases. However, CO2 is the most critical greenhouse gas due to its large
scale of emission. Global warming drives climate change, causing more
severe and frequent weather conditions, melting ice caps, and other
impacts. Reducing greenhouse gas emissions is crucial for mitigating
climate change, limiting global warming, improving air quality, and
ensuring sustainable use of natural resources.
Energy use contributes to 73.2 % of global greenhouse gas emissions,
of which 24.2 % is emitted by the industry sector alone [1]. In 2018, the
world manufactured 1.82 Gt of crude steel, making it one of the largest
CO2-emitting sectors [2]. Specifically, direct emissions from the iron and
steel sector accounted for 2.6 Gt of CO2, representing approximately 7 %
of global CO2 emissions generated by energy systems [3]. On an average,
for each tonne of crude steel produced in 2018, the industry emitted
approximately 1.81 tonnes of CO2 and consumed around 19.51 GJ of
energy [4]. Three primary processes used for crude steel production are
the blast furnace–basic oxygen furnace process, the direct reduced
iron–electric arc furnace process, and the scrap–electric arc furnace

process with specific energy consumption of 24.4, 25.3, and 10.0 GJ per
tonne of crude steel (tcs) and specific emission intensity of 2.32, 1.65,
0.67 t CO2 per tonne of crude steel (tcs) [4]. Many processes, like rotary
kiln–induction furnace process, hearth furnaces, fluidized bed furnaces,
etc., have a small share in global production and are limited to specific
geographical locations. Identifying different CO2 abatement measures to
decarbonize the global iron and steel industry is essential.

sector. Kirschen et al. [5] studied the impact of raw materials on the
energy balance in the electric arc furnace process for steel making. Dijk
et al. [6] discussed sorption-enhanced water gas shift technology for CO2

coal-based steel production and studied the preparedness of countries
for a low-carbon transition based on specific indicators, including
implementation of low-carbon electricity and climate and energy policy.
Fan and Friedmann [9] discussed technology options and their economic
implications for decarbonizing these industries for several iron and
steel-making processes. Wang et al. [10] discussed the energy efficiency
stagnation in global steel production and called for abstinence from
primary steel-making to achieve climate targets. Tanzer et al. [11]

process and their economics. This paper presents a study about the
decarbonization of the rotary kiln-induction furnace process, as it is one

• Identification of major CO2 abatement options, categorized as energy

• Techno-economic analysis of major decarbonization measures is

• Investigated the expected decarbonization potential of major options, along with their associated costs and the impact of implementing multiple CO2 abatement options in conjunction with each
other;
• Results are presented graphically through marginal abatement and
green premium curves; and
• The extent to which decarbonization can be achieved without a net
cost to the customer is also analyzed and presented.

2. Indian iron and steel sector
The Indian iron and steel sector is the second largest in the world,
producing 125 Mt of crude steel in 2022, or 6.6 % of the worldwide
crude steel output [39]. The Indian iron and steel sector is known for its
diversity, with different production processes significantly contributing
to the overall output. Specifically, the blast furnace–basic oxygen
furnace process contributes about 44.5 % of the total production, the
rotary kiln–induction furnace process contributes 19.6 %, and the shaft
furnace–electric arc furnace process contributes 5.1 % [39]. The
remaining production is achieved through various methods, such as a
combination of blast furnaces and electric arc furnaces, electric furnaces
for scrap processing, and alternative processes like Corex. The Indian
iron and steel sector is characterized by high energy consumption,
ranging from 25.1 to 27.2 GJ/tcs [40], and significant CO2 emissions,
with an emission intensity of about 2.3 tonnes of CO2 per tonne of crude
steel [3].
Globally, India is the largest sponge iron producer, accounting for
approximately one-third of the global sponge iron production of 111 Mt
in 2019 [41]. In contrast to different regions of the world where natural
gas is predominantly used in shaft furnaces for sponge iron production,
the coal-based process contributes to 80.1 % of India’s sponge iron
production [39]. Coal is used to produce syngas in a gasifier and then
used for the reduction of iron ore lumps/pellets in a shaft furnace.
Alternatively, non-coking coal is used in rotary kilns along with iron ore
and dolomite as feed materials. These rotary kiln-based sponge iron
plants are often accompanied by a power plant to recover heat from the
high-temperature kiln-off gases having low calorific value. In the Indian
steel sector, sponge iron plants typically use induction furnaces to produce crude steel from sponge iron.
The Indian sponge iron sector is thriving primarily due to the
abundant availability of domestic high-grade iron ore, sponge iron grade
non-coking coal, low capital investment, ease of sponge iron plant
implementation, and better quality of products. Due to the local availability of raw materials, nearly 86 % of India’s rotary kiln-based sponge
iron capacity is located in five states: Odisha, Chhattishgarh, Jharkhand,
Karnataka, and West Bengal. The estimated average capacity of a sponge
iron plant is 350 tonnes per day, while the capacities of sponge iron
rotary kilns vary between 100 and 500 tonnes per day. Smaller units and
plant sizes and the use of low-grade non-coking coal are some of the
reasons for the higher energy and emission intensities of the Indian
sponge iron industry.

plants in the Indian market due to the lower carbon content in the final

3. Process description with mass and energy balances
Msponge iron = M crude steel + Mslag

The rotary kiln-based sponge iron unit consists of a rotary kiln, a
rotary cooler, a magnetic separator, and a system of conveyors for material handling. The rotary kiln is a long cylindrical rotating drum
slightly inclined to the horizontal, internally lined with refractories, and
has several port holes located along its length for air injection. Feed
material containing iron ore (hematite), feed coal, and dolomite is fed
into the rotary kiln from the feed end (higher end), and injection coal
(also called slinger coal) is injected through the discharge end (lower
end). Air is supplied through the lower end and port holes located along
the kiln length. Fig. 1 presents a schematic diagram of a rotary
kiln–induction furnace process.
Coal acts as the source of both energy and reductant (carbon).
Dolomite absorbs the sulfur and prevents contamination of sponge iron
due to sulfur in the coal. The raw materials are heated to 1000 ◦ C. The
rotation and inclination of the kiln move the solids in the kiln towards
the discharge end, while the pressure difference causes the motion of
gases towards the feed end, creating a counter-current flow. Rotation of
the kiln mixes the feed material and traps counter-current moving air to
form reducing gas (carbon monoxide, CO) that reduces the iron ore
(Fe2O3) to metallic iron. Through this counter-current arrangement, a
solid mixture containing sponge iron, calcined dolomite, coal char, and
ash emerges from the rotary kiln through the discharge end, and waste
gases leave through the feed end. Subsequently, the solid mixture is
cooled in the rotary cooler to 110 ◦ C, followed by the separation of
sponge iron using a magnetic separator. Cooling of sponge iron serves
two purposes: (i) prevents reoxidation of sponge iron, and (ii) enables
separation of sponge iron through a magnetic separator (iron has poor
magnetic properties at high temperatures). Sponge iron from the magnetic separator is melted in an induction furnace to make steel. Dolochar (mixture of calcined dolomite and coal char) can either be combusted in the captive power plant after blending with high volatile
matter coal or sold to the market for energy uses or used as a landfill.
Heat is recovered via the combustion of unburnt hydrocarbons, contained in the kiln off-gas, in a waste heat recovery boiler to generate
electricity.

4.2. Variable frequency drive
The rotary kiln uses both iron ore lumps and iron ore pellets as feed
material. The typical capacity utilization of the sponge iron-making unit
is 90 % with iron ore lumps. Variable frequency drives can help reduce
electricity consumption as equipment running at 90 % of the rated speed
consumes only 73 % of the rated power [44]. Motors power the
conveyor system, rotation of the rotary kiln and rotary cooler, coal
cutter, screens, compressors, and pumps. It is assumed that 90 % of the
electricity is consumed by motors. The amount of CO2 saved is computed
by multiplying the electricity savings with the grid emission intensity
(see Table 3). The negative carbon abatement cost of − 93.7 $/t CO2
signifies a net saving associated with the implementation of this option.

4.4. Better-grade dolomite
Dolomite helps in absorbing coal sulfur in the rotary kiln. Otherwise,
coal sulfur is absorbed by sponge iron and results in contamination. Note
that dolomite gets calcined due to the high temperature inside the rotary
kiln and releases CO2. Calcined dolomite contains calcium oxide (CaO),
which absorbs sulfur. However, the share of calcium oxide in limestone/
dolomite varies from 20 % to 60 %, depending on its source of origin
[45]. The use of better-grade dolomite in rotary kilns is helpful in
decreasing the CO2 emissions due to calcination. A positive carbon
abatement cost of 51.7 $/t CO2 signifies a net investment associated
with the implementation of this option.

4.3. Gravimetric separator
In a traditional rotary kiln process, sponge iron comes out of the
rotary kiln at about 1020 ◦ C and is then cooled to 110 ◦ C in a rotary
cooler before being separated from a solid mixture containing sponge
iron, coal ash, and dolo-char mixture. Sponge iron is then stored and
later used in the steel-making process. The sponge iron is reactive and
the heat recovery from the rotary cooler is avoided in a conventional
plant to prevent its reoxidation. The rotary cooler can be eliminated, and

4.5. Biochar replacing feed coal
High temperature inside the rotary kiln results in the conversion of
feed coal to coal char. Due to the reactivity of char and deficient air
supply, carbon monoxide (CO) is formed which helps in the iron ore
reduction process. Feed coal can be replaced by biochar obtained from
woody biomass. Heating woody biomass in the absence of air produces
biochar and other combustible products. Due to the higher reactivity of
biochar to form carbon monoxide as compared to coal char [46], biochar
can fully replace feed coal in the rotary kiln process. Feed coal is
substituted by biochar on an energy-equivalent basis. Biochar from
renewable plantations eliminates the use of fossil carbon in sponge iron
and crude steel production. The use of biochar in rotary kilns decreases
the quantity of unburnt hydrocarbons in the after-burning chamber,
which further leads to a decrease in the power output from the power
plant. However, combustible byproducts generated during the charring
of woody biomass are combusted to generate power resulting in a net
increase in power by 378.7 kWh/t of crude steel. The use of biochar in a
rotary kiln does not pose any significant technological challenges [47].
It was also tested on a pilot scale by one of the Indian steel producers.
However, this option faced challenges as the supply chain for biomass is
not yet well developed due to its distributed and seasonal availability.
The amount of CO2 savings is calculated by adding the emissions

4.9. Green hydrogen replacing slinger coal
A rotary kiln primarily uses coal as a reductant. Coal is fed into the
rotary kiln through two different streams, feed coal and slinger coal.
Feed coal goes into the kiln along with iron ore and dolomite through the
feed end. Due to the rotary motion of the kiln (~0.5 rpm) and inclination
with the horizontal (2.5–3 %), the feed materials get mixed together and
move towards the discharge end. The rotary kiln has two phases:
oxidizing gas phase and reducing bed phase. In the gas phase, volatiles
and carbon monoxide react to form carbon dioxide. Due to the rotary
motion of the kiln, CO2 is trapped in the bed phase, where it reacts with
solid carbon to form carbon monoxide, and reduces Fe2O3 to metallic
iron. Owing to this process requirement, feed coal is not replaced. On the
other hand, slinger coal is fed to the kiln through the discharge end to
maintain a high temperature inside the rotary kiln. In this study, the
slinger coal is replaced with green hydrogen for decarbonization.
A methodology for calculating the CO2 abatement is discussed in the
following section considering the interdependency of the various
decarbonization options.

4.6. Char recycling
The magnetic separator separates sponge iron and dolo-char (a
mixture of calcined dolomite and char). Dolo-char can be separated
using a gravimetric separator, and the coal char can be recycled as a feed
material replacing an equivalent amount of feed coal. The amount of
feed coal replaced is calculated by equating the fixed carbon in coal char
with fixed carbon in coal. Reduction in feed coal decreases the CO2
emissions, however, it also decreases the power output from the waste
heat recovery and power plant. The power output is decreased because
feed coal releases volatile matter, most of which goes to the waste heat
recovery boiler uncombusted. It is assumed that only 90 % of the char is
separated from the dolo-char mixture. The amount of CO2 saved is
calculated by adding the emissions avoided due to decreased coal usage,
the amount of CO2 released from char combustion, and the CO2 emission
equivalent due to power loss in the power plant. The power required to
separate char from the dolo-char mixture is also accounted for as power
loss. The operation cost is computed by subtracting the cost of total
power lost from the cost of coal saved due to the implementation of char
recycling. Char recycling is not a CO2 abatement option when implemented independently (see Table 3) because the direct carbon abatement is just 26.5 kg CO2 with a power loss of 35.2 kWh/tcs. This power
loss is compensated by grid electricity and results in a net increase in
CO2 emissions by 2.4 kg/t of crude steel. However, in the case of combined implementation of several decarbonization options (as discussed
later), power purchase from the grid is eliminated, and direct CO2
abatement becomes equal to total CO2 abatement.

5. Abatement and green premium curves
Any two decarbonization measures can either be independent (i.e.,
they do not impact each other), dependent (i.e., they may impact each
other but they can be implemented simultaneously), or mutually
exclusive (i.e., they cannot be implemented simultaneously). This
interdependency plays a major role in determining the abatement cost
curve for decarbonization. Krishna Priya et al. [48] described an algorithm to arrange such options. Another methodology to determine the
abatement cost curve with such constrained options is discussed as
follows.
5.1. Methodology
A flowchart, highlighting the sorting of decarbonization options, is
presented in Fig. 4. To visualize the effect of all options, the complete
algorithm needs to be reiterated with each set of mutually exclusive
options such that none of the options in a particular set are mutually
exclusive. In the case of dependent options, only the marginal effect is
assigned to the option implemented later. Marginal values of cost and
emission are calculated by subtracting the impact of the better option
(with lower CO2 abatement cost) from the combined implementation of
both options.
At the beginning of the sorting algorithm, two sets are assumed. The
first set (set A) has all the decarbonization options placed in it and the
second set (set B) is empty. At the beginning of the first cycle, all the
options in set A are sorted in increasing order of the cost of carbon
abatement. The best option is moved to set B. The cost and CO2 abatement of each option in set A is reevaluated considering the selection of
options transferred to set B. If any option in set A is mutually exclusive to
options in set B, that option is discarded. If an option in set A is independent of options in set B, then the cost and CO2 abatement for that
option does not change. Subsequently, the options in set A are again
sorted to identify the option with the least carbon abatement cost and
the cycle is repeated till the last option in set A is moved to set B. During
each cycle of repeating the algorithm, one option is moved from set A to
set B. The algorithm ends when the set A becomes empty.
All the sorted options are used to plot the carbon abatement cost
curve. If an option does not have any effective carbon abatement but
decreases the cost, it is removed while generating the marginal abatement cost curve. However, it may still be a part of the green premium
curve as discussed later.

4.7. Bio-syngas replacing slinger coal
Slinger (injection) coal maintains the temperature inside the rotary
kiln. It can, therefore, be replaced with gaseous fuel for higher combustion efficiency and decreasing the net energy input. Biomass can be
gasified in a gasifier, and the bio-syngas can be injected through the
discharge end instead of slinger coal. This option eliminates the use of
slinger coal and also decreases the power output from the power plant
because higher combustion efficiency of gaseous fuel decreases the
quantity of uncombusted kiln off-gas going into the power plant. The use
of coal syngas has been successfully tested by small sponge iron plants.
However, it is not practiced at present due to significant differences in
the run time of the gasifier compared to the campaign life of a rotary
kiln. Renewable biomass is a carbon-neutral option, and hence, the use
of bio-syngas to replace coal is considered a decarbonization option.
4.8. Natural gas replacing slinger coal
Coal is a solid fuel and has poor combustion efficiency as compared
to natural gas. Further, natural gas has lower CO2 emissions per unit of
energy than coal. This makes natural gas an excellent alternative to
slinger coal. Similar to the bio-syngas option, it also decreases power
output from the power plant due to the decreased quantity of uncombusted/partially combusted components in the kiln off-gas. Typical
continuous run-time (also called campaign life) of a rotary kiln is usually
110 days as compared to the campaign life of gasifiers which is in the
range of 20 days. Due to this, the implementation of gasifier-based
carbon abatement options poses operational issues. Natural gas injection to replace slinger coal does not have any such operational problems
and can be implemented easily and reliably. However, natural gas
provides lower CO2 abatement as compared to carbon-neutral biosyngas.

furnace process. The results indicate that the choice of decarbonization
pathways can impact emission reduction. Near-complete decarbonization of the coal-based sponge iron production and subsequent crude
steel manufacturing has a very high green premium in the range of

separator for product separation.
The proposed decarbonization measures include improved energy
efficiency, improved material efficiency, use of renewable energy, and
fuel substitution. Through a combination of these measures, a substantial decarbonization of sponge iron industries can be achieved. By
exploring the decarbonization potential, associated costs, and the synergistic effects of implementing multiple abatement options, this
research contributes to the development of informed policy decisions.
The insights gained from this research provide a foundation for
designing and implementing a net-zero pathway for the sector. By
incorporating the identified decarbonization strategies, policymakers
can effectively mitigate the environmental impact of the sponge iron
industry while promoting sustainable growth and resource efficiency.
Industrial symbiosis can play a significant role in the decarbonization of
the entire sector directly (e.g., dolo-char can also be used in the cement
industry and can be helpful in decreasing otherwise emissions from
calcination in clinker units) or indirectly (e.g., purchase of carbon
credits). Future studies can delve deeper into specific technologies,
explore potential barriers and enablers for implementation, and assess
the long-term economic and environmental impacts of adopting various
decarbonization options, including industrial symbiosis.
