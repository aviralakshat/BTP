A B S T R A C T

Handling Editor: Kathleen Aviso

The energy-intensive steel industry is responsible for >7% of global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, of which
the process of ironmaking contributes a large majority. Direct Reduced Iron (DRI) manufacturing, a growing
technology in ironmaking, offers the potential to reduce emissions. India, as the largest manufacturer of DRI, is
characterized by technological diversity, while simultaneously dominated by coal-based DRI processes. This
study develops a comprehensive cradle-to-gate (raw material to iron product) life cycle assessment of three
distinct technological pathways for producing DRI: direct coal reduction in a rotary kiln (rotary kiln DRI process), coal gasifier and shaft furnace (coal gasifier DRI process), and reformed natural gas and shaft furnace (NG
reformer DRI process). A hybrid life cycle inventory approach is employed, involving process modeling, literature data, and industry consultation to establish baseline process metrics, followed by a sensitivity analysis to
address the impact of variabilities and uncertainties. Depending on the supply chain and process assumptions,
emissions attribution approach to multi-product systems, and the specific choice of inputs and outputs, the life
cycle GHG emissions associated with DRI production range from 1391.1 to 1880.0 kgCO2e/t-DRI for the rotary
kiln DRI process, 1565.5 to 1969.1 kgCO2e/t-DRI for the coal gasifier DRI process, and 815.3 to 1160.1 kgCO2e/
t-DRI for the NG reformer DRI process. While the research considers India as a case study, the methodology and
results can be directionally and broadly applicable to other geographies and process conditions.

1. Introduction
The iron and steel industry accounts for >7% (IEA, 2020) of the
global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, of which the energy-intensive
ironmaking process (crucial to primary steelmaking) contributes the
vast majority. Further, the share of primary steelmaking (steel produced
from iron ore) in global steel output exceeds 80%, while scrap recycling
meets about 20% of worldwide demand (IEA, 2020). The major processes for primary steelmaking are the Blast Furnace (BF) – Basic oxygen
furnace (BOF), Direct Reduced Iron (DRI) – Electric Arc Furnace (EAF),
and Smelt Reduction – BOF routes. The BF-BOF route is primarily
coal-based and has a reported overall emissions intensity of 2.2 tonne (t)
CO2/tonne of crude steel (TCS) (IEA, 2020). DRI is characterized by a
portfolio of diverse technology options consisting broadly of coal-based
and gas-based processes. The CO2 emissions associated with the natural
gas (NG) DRI-EAF route are 1.4 t-CO2/TCS, while scrap based steel

production is reported to have an emissions intensity of 0.3 t-CO2/TCS
(IEA, 2020) due to the absence of emissions involving the
energy-intensive ironmaking step. Recently, Fan and Friedmann (2021)
have reviewed various low-carbon technology pathways in iron and
steelmaking, citing carbon emission values based on prior literature.
Notably, the messages from these macro-level studies do not have the
benefit of detailed life cycle assessment (LCA) for various DRI pathways.
India’s steel sector is unique in technology diversity - integrated (BFBOF) process co-exists with significant DRI capacity. The Indian ironmaking industry is one of the more GHG-intensive globally, with a noted
emissions intensity of about 2.3 t-CO2/TCS - well above the global
average of 1.85 t-CO2/TCS (IEA, 2020; WSA, 2020a). Heavy reliance on
coal, lower-grade iron ore and process inefficiencies are offered as primary reasons for the high emissions intensity. As the second-largest
steel-producing country, India produced 110 million tonnes (MT) of
steel in 2019 (~6% of global output) (WSA, 2020b). Further, production

unavailability of scrap and low per-capita steel consumption levels, a
majority of the future steel demand would be met through primary
production. As a fast-growing economy with a large base capacity, India
offers a unique opportunity to mitigate emissions from primary steelmaking through the adoption of low-carbon technologies and feedstock.
This requires an in-depth discussion of the key factors contributing to
GHG emissions in various ironmaking technologies. More specifically, a
critical analysis, using India as a case study, of emissions-reduction
pathways in DRI manufacturing may be especially beneficial, given
the significant potential of this technology space towards low-carbon
steelmaking technologies of the future.
Material and energy flow for several pathways in the iron and steel
industry, including BF-BOF (Griffina et al., 2019), DRI-EAF (Dey et al.,
2015), hydrogen-based DRI or H-DRI (Vogl et al., 2018) and hydrogen
injection (Yilmaz et al., 2017) in blast furnaces (Tang et al., 2021) have
been discussed. An accumulative model was developed for the life cycle
comparison of energy, resources and waste factors (Li et al., 2002), but
the study does not cover GHG emission factors. On sponge iron-making
in coal-based rotary kilns, there are studies on process modeling (Gaurav
and Khanam, 2016), energy efficiency analysis (Dey et al., 2015), heat
loss minimization (Chakrabarti et al., 2016), and benchmarking (CSIR
CIMFR, 2015). Jin et al. (2017) investigated the life cycle emissions from
the BF-BOF process. Direct CO2 emissions associated with DRI production using natural gas (NG), coal-derived syngas, and coke oven gas
making have been discussed (Sarkar et al., 2018).
To the best of authors’ knowledge, there is no rigorous LCA study
comparing various coal and natural gas (NG)-based DRI technologies in
practice today - coal-based rotary kiln DRI, coal gasification-based DRI
and NG-based DRI manufacturing, even though these options are likely
to dominate the DRI technological choices of the future, especially for
India. This study attempts to fill the existing gap by presenting a
comprehensive, systematic, and comparative LCA of the following three
routes for DRI production: i) coal-based rotary kiln, ii) coal gasificationbased shaft furnace (hereafter referred to as coal gasifier DRI process),
and iii) NG-based shaft furnace (hereafter referred to as NG reformer
DRI process. The study uses a unique hybrid approach that combines
first-principles-based modeling for material and energy balances with
data validation through industry expert consultations, and rigorous
implementation of Life Cycle Inventory (LCI) from established databases
and publications. A detailed GHG inventory is provided, covering all the
life cycle stages of DRI production, from upstream processes (i.e., from
extraction and processing of raw materials and fuels) to transportation
and use of these materials in a DRI plant. It is expected that the insights
from applying this consistent, uniform basis to compare the GHG
emissions of the above-mentioned three routes for producing DRI will
spur further research interest in low-carbon DRI technologies (e.g., HDRI) and enable informed policy decisions regarding future trajectories

for mitigating emissions from the global steelmaking industry in general
and the Indian steel industry in particular.
2. Methodology for life cycle assessment
Recognizing DRI as the uniform primary output from all three processes, the sections below discuss system definition, major assumptions
and scenarios explored.
2.1. Scope and system boundaries
Representative processes for rotary kiln, coal gasifier, and NG
reformer processes for producing DRI are described in Section S2.2 of
the Supplementary Material. The functional unit of the study is one
tonne of DRI (t-DRI) produced in the plant. As shown in Fig. 1, the study
quantifies the “cradle-to-gate” GHG impacts of DRI manufacturing,
covering all stages from raw materials (iron ore and dolomite) and fuel
extraction (coal and natural gas) to DRI production, following best
practices (ISO 14040, 2006; ISO 14044, 2006). The cumulative GHG
(CO2, CH4, and N2O) emissions across the DRI value chain are quantified
as CO2-equivalents (CO2eq) using the 100-year Global Warming Potential (GWP-100) values (IPCC, 2013).
2.2. Base case assumptions and other scenarios
Table 1 lists input data and parameters that are potential sources of

Based on industry data and literature, appropriate grades of feed and
injection coal are assumed for the base case processes (Supplementary
Material S4.1). Lump ore is considered for the rotary kiln process (Sarangi and Sarangi, 2011), whereas the shaft furnace-based processes use
pellets (Chatterjee, 2014). The base case for the coal gasifier DRI process
uses low ash coal for gasification, and a Top Gas Recycle Ratio (TGRR) of
85% (Section S4.2). Using Sensitivity analysis enables testing the effect
of parameters listed in Table 1 on the GHG emissions. For the NG
reformer DRI process, the base case uses lean gas with a 98.3 vol%
methane content and a TGRR of 65% (Supplementary Material S4.3).

multiple products, attribution of emissions to DRI and other valuable byproducts is carried out using ISO 14044 (ISO 14040, 2006; ISO 14044,
2006) recommendations. For the coal gasifier DRI process, allocation of
emissions is avoided by using process partitioning–a method that considers only the segments of the process involved in generating the
by-products, but excludes the rest of the operations unconnected to their
production. However, the rotary kiln process may generate electricity as

3. Life cycle inventory
The study uses a combination of relevant literature data and process
modeling (using Aspen Plus and Microsoft Excel), validation by
consultation with industry experts, and generic data from the Ecoinvent
database (Wernet et al., 2016) to generate relevant LCI. Peer-reviewed
studies are the first choice of data selection; for example, coal and NG
extraction and supply chain data can be reliably sourced from LCA
literature. Where complete inventory data is unavailable (e.g., mass,
energy and emissions inventory), as is the case for DRI processes, data
generated from process modeling is used and validated with inputs from
industry experts and other reports. For national grid inventories,
Ecoinvent database (obtained via SimaPro software) is used. The GHG
inventories for feed and fuel sourcing, industry clusters and transportation pathways are presented below.

3.1. Feed and fuel sourcing

The sponge iron industry in India uses a mix of domestic and imported iron ore, coal and dolomite. The study considers emissions from
diesel and electricity used for mining, beneficiation, transportation and
shipping for all raw material and fuel. Emissions from coal mining across
India (Mallapragada et al., 2019) and other countries are estimated by

emissions factors (for India, Australia, Indonesia and South Africa).
Literature data is used to estimate emissions from iron ore mining in
South Africa (Kumbka, 2019), Australia (Haque and Norgate, 2015) and
India (IBM, 2014–15, NMDC, 2018). Emissions from ore-to-pellet conversion are also included in the analysis (KIOCL, 2019). Inventory data
on dolomite mining are calculated from mine statistics published by the
Indian Government (DGMS, 2015). LNG import from the Marcellus shale
region in the US (Laurenzi and Jersey, 2013) and associated LCI (Mallapragada et al., 2019) are considered for this study. A detailed
description of fuel and feed sourcing can be found in the Supplementary

3.3.1. Rotary kiln DRI process data and analysis
Modeling data indicates a specific energy requirement of 21.9 GJ/tDRI (HHV basis) from coal fed to the kiln (Fig. 2, Fig. S6, Supplementary
Material). Heat losses in the kiln include the furnace skin (2.2 GJ/t-DRI),
DRI product’s sensible heat (4.1 GJ/t-DRI) losses, and the char’s sensible
heat (0.26 GJ/t-DRI) and chemical energy (1.95 GJ/t-DRI). The kiln offgas carries 4.1 GJ/t-DRI of energy content, which is used for producing
451 kWh/t-DRI electricity in a waste heat recovery (WHR) unit. About
100 kWh/t-DRI is consumed at the plant for meeting the auxiliary load,
whereas the residual 351 kWh/t-DRI is assumed to be exported to the
grid. The remaining input energy is used to drive various chemical reactions in the kiln. More discussion on process energetics is reported in
the Supplementary Material (Section S5.8).

3.3.3. NG reformer DRI process data and analysis
The NG requirements for iron ore reduction vary slightly with
composition (Table 3). In the base case, 180.1 kg/t-DRI (11.06 kmol/tDRI or 265.4 Nm3/t-DRI) of lean gas is required to generate syngas. In
the alternative rich gas case, an additional 1.3 kg NG is needed. A
suitable TGRR must be chosen to generate an acceptable syngas quality
(H2/CO ratio >1.6) (Chatterjee, 2014) required for iron ore reduction
and to supply the thermal energy needs. The total specific energy
requirement for the lean gas case is 10.98 GJ HHV/t-DRI (reduced by
0.1% upon switching to rich gas), showing that the energetics of the
process may not be significantly affected by NG composition (Fig. 3).

Upstream and plant criteria emissions of SOx, NOx and Particulate
matter (PM) are reported based on literature estimates. While assuming
that industries adhere to emissions regulation wherever mandated by
norms, this study considers uncontrolled criteria emissions for unregulated sectors. Plant-level emissions are calculated by mass balance and
actual measurements indicated by UNEP (1983), CPCB (2007) and US
EPA (1998).
Unlike GHG emissions that have a global footprint, criteria pollutants
have a local impact. Therefore, this study lists unregulated criteria
pollutants emitted to the atmosphere at various phases. Details on the
methodology and approach are expounded in the Supplementary Material (Section S5.11). In general, criteria pollutant emissions across
upstream processes are proportional to the energy use and the respective
GHG emissions.

4.4. Sensitivity analysis
Noting that the sources of variability in the rotary kiln DRI process
(Fig. 6A) span the supply and process chains, the following parameters
are most sensitive to the GHG emission intensity of DRI: emissions
attribution to the electricity by-product generated via waste heat recovery (18.2%), iron ore pelletization (15.9%), sourcing of coal and iron
ore from different geographies (6.7–13.4%), VM-C combustion in the
kiln (− 12.5%), waste heat recovery efficiency (− 6.5%–6%) and injection coal quality (− 1.2%–4.7%). Integrating the rotary kiln process with
a waste heat recovery unit significantly impacts the life cycle GHG intensity. With no waste heat recovery, the GHG intensity increases to
1880 kgCO2e/t-DRI. Similarly, the captive power unit’s net efficiency
has a significant impact on the life cycle GHG emissions of the coal kiln
process. Using pelletized ores instead of lump ores increases the net life
cycle GHGs by ~16%. The life cycle GHG emissions of the rotary kiln
DRI process are sensitive to the process modeling uncertainty relating to
combustion of the feed coal’s volatile matter (VM-C) in the kiln. If the
VM-C burns in the kiln, it significantly reduces the plant’s feed and injection coal consumption, thus also reducing the net life GHG cycle
emissions from 1590 to 1391 kgCO2e/t-DRI. Using domestically sourced
raw material provides significant GHG emission benefits relative to
sourcing coal, iron ore, or dolomite from abroad.

process is highly sensitive to a set of parameters related to sourcing and
transport of coal and iron ore, specifically mining, iron ore pelletization,
and inter-country shipping and transport. A GHG reduction of 252.4

Depending on the supply chain and process assumptions, emission
attribution to by-products, and the specific choice of inputs and outputs,
the life cycle GHG emissions associated with DRI production range from
1391 to 1880 kgCO2e/t-DRI for the rotary kiln DRI process, 1566 to
1969 kgCO2e/t-DRI for the coal gasifier DRI process, and 815 to 1160
kgCO2e/t-DRI for the NG reformer DRI process. With the range of uncertainties and variabilities considered, sensitivity analysis results show
that the NG reformer DRI process has the potential to lower life cycle
GHG footprint of DRI from the coal-based processes by 17–59%. The
rotary kiln DRI process offers 11% and 4.5% lower GHG emissions on the
lowest and highest end of the sensitivity band, when compared to the
coal gasifier DRI process. However, if ore pelletization is uniformly
considered for all DRI process, the emissions of the coal gasifier process
could be up to 7.7% lower than the rotary kiln process (higher range).
Therefore, the choice of operational conditions and supply chain parameters determines which coal-based DRI has the lowest GHG
footprint.
Even though the process emissions are lower for the coal gasification
process than the rotary kiln, the life cycle emissions from the former are
higher than the latter due to the imported electricity and iron ore
pelletization requirements. This underscores the importance of uniform,
consistent approaches and application of LCA as a tool for quantifying
the GHG impacts of different fuel and technology options on a holistic
cradle-to-gate basis.
The study can be further extended to refine the process energetics by
considering reaction kinetics aspects of the three routes considered in
the analysis. Further, a comparative techno-economic analysis can be
done to compare the cost-competitiveness of the three processes
considered for the analysis. Approaches in this study can also provide a
consistent and uniform basis to assess the emissions reduction potential
of Hydrogen-based DRI (H-DRI) processes, projected to be a long-term,
deep GHG reduction solution for iron and steelmaking. Specifically, HDRI studies require significant system-level assessment of blue, gray and
green hydrogen pathways that merit a separate study. On a life cycle
emissions basis, this study establishes that technologies like NG reformer
DRI offer significant potential for GHG emissions reduction relative to
coal-based processes for DRI manufacture in India. The economic, sociopolitical, and policy implications of the technology transition to lowercarbon production processes of the future lie outside the scope of this
study, and also merit separate discussion.
