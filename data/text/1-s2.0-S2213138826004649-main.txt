The iron and steel sector contributes nearly 7% of the global energy-related CO2 emissions. In India, this challenge is amplified due to high production of coal based direct reduced iron (DRI) (~80% share). This research
evaluates the techno-economic feasibility of hydrogen-based direct reduction (H2–DRI) for India’s steel industry
and benchmarks its results against natural gas-based DRI (NG–DRI), under realistic market conditions. A 1
million tons per annum (MTPA) H2–DRI plant and NG-DRI plant is modeled using ASPEN Plus v14, and economic
estimates are developed with sensitivity to renewable electricity tariffs, load factor, and policy levers such as
carbon pricing and green steel premiums. By comparing DRI based on natural gas, hydrogen and coal the impact
of capital expenditure, operational expenditure and overall economics in terms of levelized cost of iron production is evaluated. Results indicate that demand side dynamics like green steel premium and carbon pricing
are important factors towards economic viability of H2 based DRI.

Introduction
The world encounters the challenge posed by continued greenhouse
gas emissions which are projected to increase global warming, with
scenarios indicating a likely rise in the global average temperature this
century to 1.5◦ C above pre-industrial levels [1]. In 2024, global energy
related CO2 emissions were approximately 37.8 gigatons, showcasing
the magnitude of efforts required in achieving net-zero emission targets
[2]. The iron and steel sector remains one of the most carbon intensive
sectors, emitting ~ 2.6 Gt CO2 annually, equivalent of ~ 7% of global
energy system emissions [3]. In this scenario, conventional steel
manufacturing, an important activity for industrial growth emits on an
average of 2.18 t CO2 per ton (t) of crude steel, with energy consumption
of ~ 21 GJ/t. The share of renewable energy in iron and steel production
is below 2%, and several efforts are in place to further increase its
penetration [4].
It is widely accepted that decarbonization of the iron and steel

industry requires a strategic approach which addresses both process
efficiency and fuel substitution. Energy efficiency improvements, such
as advanced furnace technologies and waste heat recovery, have the
promise to deliver immediate reductions in energy intensity [5].
Increasing scrap utilization through Electric Arc Furnace (EAF) routes
further lowers emissions by reducing reliance on virgin iron ore [5].
Transitioning from coal-based rotary kiln DRI to Natural Gas-based DRI
(NG-DRI) offers a pragmatic intermediate step, cutting CO2 emissions by
nearly 50–70% compared to coal-based routes while leveraging existing
shaft furnace infrastructure, specifically in developing countries like
India [6].
In the long term, Hydrogen-based Direct Reduction (H2-DRI) represents a near-zero carbon pathway, with lifecycle emissions below 0.1 t
CO2 per ton of steel when powered by renewable electricity [7]. These
measures can further be augmented by Carbon Capture, Utilization, and
Storage (CCUS) through which residual emissions in Blast Furnace-Basic
Oxygen Furnace (BF-BOF) and transitional DRI processes can be further

Replacing coal with hydrogen in shaft furnaces is an important step
towards achieving the goal of net-zero ironmaking [9]. When hydrogen
is produced via renewable-powered electrolysis and process heat is fully
electrified, CO2 emissions can be significantly reduced. Hydrogen based
iron-oxide reduction also yields ultra-pure iron with no dissolved carbon, differentiating it from conventional pig iron [10,11]. The technical
feasibility of this approach has been further demonstrated by global
projects such as MIDREX-H2 and HYBRIT [12–14].
However, there are major economic barriers persisting in deployment of hydrogen-based steel production on a commercial scale:

relevance in emissions intensity tracking and target–setting. On the
technology front, HYBRIT has produced more than 5000 tonnes (t) of
hydrogen–reduced iron at pilot scale and submitted its six–year results
(2018–2024) before moving to the next industrial implementation phase
which serves as evidence that H2–DRI is beyond laboratory maturity and
entering scale–up [28]. Future projections in countries like China
envisage adoption of H2 based DRI technology, highlighting its potential
as sustainable low carbon route for steel making and projecting 20% to
30% hydrogen based total steel production by 2060 [29,30].
Several studies have examined the thermodynamics and kinetics of
hydrogen-based DRI (H2-DRI), highlighting its potential as a near-zero
carbon solution [31,32]. Process modeling of H2-DRI approaches vary
from open-source Python-based mass and energy flow models [10] to
ASPEN Plus simulations for green hydrogen production and integration
into DRI plants [33]. Facility-level assessments have quantified energy
consumption, greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, and cost implications
for electrically driven H2-DRI plants using water electrolysis [34–36].
Gurgel et al. [37] demonstrate through multiple scenarios that a CO2
price greater than US$100 per t is required to reduce sectoral emissions
by more than 40 %. It also demonstrates that H2-DRI will be a dominant
pathway for future scenario at a green hydrogen price less than US$1 per
kg, and substantial reduction in CO2 emissions could be possibly achieved by switching fuel from coal to natural gas.
According to the life-cycle assessment conducted by Nduagu et al.
(2022) [38], greenhouse gas emissions associated with coal-based DRI
production range from 1.4 to 2.0 t CO2e/t-DRI, while NG-DRI production emits 0.8 to 1.1 t CO2e/t-DRI. Therefore, the adoption of NG-based
DRI offers a significant reduction in life-cycle GHG emissions relative to
coal-based DRI pathways. Lifecycle analyses also confirm that green
hydrogen-based DRI has the promise to achieve emissions below 0.1 t
CO2 per t steel, positioning it as a near-zero carbon pathway [39].
Recent techno-economic studies emphasize that electricity cost,
specific energy consumption or electrolyzer efficiency, and load factor
are dominant drivers of hydrogen production economics [33,40].
Sensitivity analyses show that increasing load factor significantly reduces the Levelized Cost of Hydrogen (LCOH), while high renewable
tariffs and low utilization rates undermine competitiveness [31]. A
comparative study between electrolyzer technology Proton Exchange
Membrane (PEM) and Alkaline Water Electrolysis (AWE) demonstrates
the trade off, in terms of initial capital investment vs system efficiency
[41]. In a detailed analysis Rosner et al.[7] have proposed that using
renewable H2 for both iron ore reduction and process heating can cut
direct emissions by ~ 85%, with economic viability around US$1.6–1.7
per kg H2. Furthermore, they have recommended to consider optimizing
top–gas recycle pressure from shaft furnace and H2 excess ratios as
process operation options to improve energy use and costs.
Cost modeling for electrolyzer technologies (proton exchange
membrane electrolyzer (PEMEL) and Alkaline Electrolyzer (AEL) predicts substantial CAPEX reductions by 2030, with economies of scale
delivering major cost benefits up to ~ 10 MW plant size [42]. Further
studies have explored integrated hydrogen utilization pathways in
ironmaking, combining process modeling with techno-economic and
environmental assessment. Kim et al. (2021) [43] demonstrated that a
Blast Furnace integrated with a Solid Oxide Electrolysis Cell (BF–SOEC)
integration can achieve significant CO2 reduction, with economic
viability strongly dependent on hydrogen production costs. Kim et al.
(2026) [44] highlighted the role of alternative hydrogen pathways such
as methane pyrolysis and system-level integration. These insights reinforce the importance of hydrogen supply pathways in techno-economic
evaluation of H2-DRI systems.

reach to the end industrial customer. It is also essential that imported
liquefied natural gas (LNG) is available at competitive and stable price

India is the world’s second-largest steel producer, expected to supply
~ 20% of global steel by 2050 [22]. Its production structure is dominated by coal-based rotary kiln DRI (~80%), making it one of the most
carbon-intensive globally [23,24]. Emission intensities of coal-based
plants often exceed 2.5 t CO2/t steel, compared to the global average
of ~ 1.8 t CO2/t [8]. The Indian iron ore and non-coking coal is
concentrated in five states (Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Karnataka, and West Bengal) which results in low capital costs and constitutes
86% of country’s sponge iron capacity [25,26].
The Ministry of Steel’s 2024 roadmap outlines pathways for green
transition, including energy efficiency, renewable integration, and
gradual integration of hydrogen based technologies [25]. The recently
introduced taxonomy for green steel defines thresholds for CO2 intensity
and sets benchmarks for “percentage greenness” rating system, which
helps in creating benchmarking and certification.
The National Green Hydrogen Mission and Ministry of New and
Renewable Energy MNRE’s 2024 guidelines have sanctioned pilot projects for hydrogen use in DRI and Blast Furnace (BF) injections, backed
by ~ US$ 50 million funding for scale up and technical feasibility [27].
Think tanks in India like IRADe highlight the dominance of coal-based
DRI, limited domestic gas availability, and the need for hybrid renewable power purchase agreements (PPAs) to ensure high electrolyzer
utilization [6].
Recognizing this challenge, India launched the National Green
Hydrogen Mission and pilot projects for H2DRI, alongside Round-TheClock Power renewable auctions to ensure firm renewable supply to
achieve a net-zero target by 2070 [16,27].
This article hence focuses on a techno-economic assessment of
hydrogen-based direct reduction (H2-DRI) for the Indian steel industry
and is benchmarked against natural gas-based DRI (NG-DRI), by
modeling a 1 MTPA plant under Indian economic conditions.

higher scrap use and selective carbon capture utilization and sequestration (CCUS). The World Steel Association’s Sustainability Indicators
2024 report [5] defines performance metrics that explicitly recognize

H2 is supplied to the reduction process after compression, purification, and preheating (~800–1000 ◦ C). The reduction gas, consisting of
H2, reacts with descending iron ore in a shaft furnace in a countercurrent configuration. The reduction mechanism is represented using
stoichiometric reactor blocks simulating stepwise conversion of iron
oxides (Fe2O3(s) → Fe3O4(s) → FeO(s) → Fe(s) ), based on literaturereported kinetics and thermodynamic feasibility [32,50,51]. Thermodynamic properties are evaluated using the Peng–Robinson equation of
state. A hydrogen excess ratio is maintained to ensure a near-complete
reduction to metallic iron and to sustain the thermodynamic driving
force.
A closed-loop configuration is adopted wherein the top gas, consisting of unreacted hydrogen and water vapor, is cooled, dewatered in a
flash unit, and partially recycled to the reactor inlet after recompression
and reheating. This recycle loop improves hydrogen utilization and
overall process efficiency. Material and energy balances derived from
the Aspen model form the basis for techno-economic analysis. Material
and energy balances derived from the Aspen model (Table 1a and

The following sections present the results of simulations for a 1
million tons per annum (MTPA) H2-DRI plant and a NG-DRI plant in
India. The analysis estimates capital expenditure (CAPEX), operating
expenditure (OPEX), levelized cost of production (LCOP), and net present value (NPV). It also compares the economic performance of H2-DRI
and NG-DRI pathways and evaluates the sensitivity of results to key
parameters, including electricity tariffs, plant load factor, carbon pricing, and green premium assumptions. While these techno-economic
drivers are well established in literature, their combined application
under India-specific cost structures, electricity conditions, and policy
context remains limited, which is addressed in this study.
Methodology: Process modelling
The study adopts a techno-economic framework exemplified in
Thomassen et al. [46], which is useful in calculating annual energy
consumption and material requirements for a steel plant with a production capacity of 1 MTPA of DRI. A DRI plant of that capacity represents the scale of commercial plants being currently considered in this
study. A similar capacity techno-economic study has been conducted for
establishing a hydrogen direct reduced iron (H2-DRI) plant in New
Zealand [40]. The electrolyzer capacity and H2 requirement for 1 MTPA
plant are within the achievable scale to make this study more realistic.
Large scale green hydrogen projects emerging worldwide could cater for
the H2 demand for the DRI plant [47]. This capacity also aligns with
typical NG-DRI plant, ensuring a fair and practical comparison as
demonstrated in the supplementary material. For comparative assessment, coal-based DRI is included as a reference baseline using literaturereported data, while H2–DRI and NG–DRI are developed using Aspen
Plus process models within this study. In the second step, detailed economic analysis of the H2-DRI plant is conducted for Indian conditions to
evaluate its economic viability, including sensitivity analysis against
CAPEX, OPEX and electricity pricing. Third, a comparative assessment
of H2-DRI and NG-DRI is performed for same capacity plant using a
financial model to determine the relative competitiveness of NG-DRI
against H2-DRI. Finally, sensitivity analyses of H2-DRI are carried out
on critical variables such as carbon tax and green steel premium to assess
their impact on financial viability.

The NG–DRI process is modeled using a MIDREX-type configuration
in Aspen Plus under steady-state conditions to establish the fundamental
model for material and energy balance [54]. A schematic representation
of the NG–DRI process is provided in Fig. S3 (Supplementary Material).
Natural gas (assumed primarily as methane) is reformed in a hightemperature catalytic reformer (~900 ◦ C, Ni-based catalyst) through a
combination of steam reforming, CO2 reforming, and partial oxidation
reactions to generate a reducing gas mixture composed of CO and H2.
This gas is introduced at the base of a shaft furnace, where it reacts
counter-currently with descending iron ore at temperatures of approximately 900–950 ◦ C, leading to stepwise reduction of iron oxides
(Fe2O3(s) → Fe3O4(s) → FeO(s) → Fe(s)) (Table S4: Supplementary
Material). The process is represented by using stoichiometric reactor
blocks for both reforming and shaft furnace reduction stages, enabling
explicit simulation of reaction pathways and material flows (Fig. S4). A
closed-loop configuration is implemented, wherein the top gas is cooled,
dewatered, and partially recycled to the reformer after recompression to
maintain gas composition and improve energy efficiency. Thermodynamic properties are evaluated using the Peng–Robinson equation of
state. The simulation is initially developed under open-loop conditions
to establish baseline mass and energy balances and subsequently
extended to a closed-loop configuration to achieve convergence through
adjustment of recycle and conversion parameters. Material and energy
balances derived from the simulation (Table 1b) form the basis of
techno-economic evaluation. The model is validated against literaturereported plant data Parisi et al. [55] and Hamadeh et al. [56],
showing good agreement in gas composition and reduction performance
(Table S5-S6: Supplementary Material). Detailed reaction systems,
process flowsheets, and validation results are provided in
Supplementary Section S2 (Fig. S3–S4, Tables S4–S6).

Results and Discussion
The sensitivity analysis of Net Present Value (NPV) for 1 MTPA of H2DRI process highlights three major economic drivers: capital expenditure (CAPEX), operating expenditure (OPEX), and price of Direct
Reduced Iron (DRI) as shown in Fig. 1. The analysis shows the impact of
percentage variation in these parameters on project NPV.
Among these, DRI pricing exerts the most significant influence on
project viability. A 25% increase in DRI pricing could improve the NPV
by US$500 million. This means that a positive NPV is achievable when a
premium price for low carbon DRI is assumed, indicating that market
willingness to pay for low-carbon steel is critical for economic feasibility. While reductions in both CAPEX and OPEX improve H2-DRI
project economics, these measures alone are insufficient to make H2-DRI
profitable without providing an incentive to produce green steel.
The economic model developed in this study indicates that H2-DRI
capital expenditure is dominated by hydrogen production infrastructure, electrolyzer and balance of plants, collectively accounting for more
than 50% of the total CAPEX. This finding is consistent, with previous
findings as reported by Rosner et al. [7], who similarly identify the
electrolyzer cost as the dominant cost contributor in steel making
through H2-DRI configuration. Therefore, technological advancements
and cost reductions in electrolyzer systems are essential to enhance
competitiveness.
Similarly, the study also shows that OPEX of Hydrogen production is
strongly influenced by electricity tariffs, significantly contributing to
total operating cost. This finding is reinforced by IEA’s 2023 hydrogen
based DRI cost model study [71], which highlights electricity pricing as
the primary determinant of total operating cost. Therefore, electrolyzer
efficiency plays a pivotal role in reducing electricity consumption, and

Limitations and future work
This study presents a process-level techno-economic assessment of
NG‑DRI and H2‑DRI under India-specific conditions; however, several
limitations remain to be addressed in future. The system boundary is
restricted to direct reduced iron (DRI) production and excludes downstream steelmaking (e.g., EAF), which may influence overall economics
and emissions depending on scrap availability. The analysis assumes
steady-state operation without explicit hydrogen storage or grid backup,
which could improve utilization under renewable intermittency. In
addition, water demand for electrolysis is not explicitly modelled and
may affect feasibility in water-stressed regions. The results are based on
India-specific assumptions and therefore represent contextual insights,
though the framework is transferable to other coal-intensive economies
(e.g., China, Indonesia, South Africa). Future work should incorporate
integrated DRI–EAF systems, storage and grid-backup configurations,
water–energy interactions, and probabilistic approaches such as Monte
Carlo simulation to capture parameter uncertainties and improve
robustness of techno-economic outcomes.
Conclusions
This study developed an Aspen Plus–based techno-economic framework to evaluate the feasibility of hydrogen-based direct reduction
(H2–DRI) for India’s steel sector and benchmark its performance against
NG–DRI and coal-based DRI routes. The results demonstrate that
H2–DRI is a technically viable and strategically important pathway for
deep decarbonization of the Indian steel industry, with the potential to
significantly reduce CO2 emissions when supported by low-carbon
electricity. While current economics are influenced by higher CAPEX,
electricity costs, plant utilization, and financing conditions, sensitivity
analyses indicate clear pathways for improving competitiveness through
technology maturation, higher renewable energy utilization, and supportive policy measures. Under the base-case assumptions, positive NPV
can be achieved through a combination of carbon pricing and green steel
premiums in the range of US$ 40–60/ton, although these values should
be interpreted as indicative rather than prescriptive. In the near term,
NG–DRI offers a practical transition pathway, delivering significantly
