balance and impact assessment

A B S T R A C T

Handling Editor: Panos Seferlis

The major share of energy consumption during steel manufacturing is spent on iron making. The unavailability of
the required quantity of recyclable steel in India has made the industries depend on sponge iron (SI) for steel
manufacturing. However, 78.5% of the SI manufactured in India uses coal as an energy source. Thus, increasing
the carbon footprint of steel manufactured in India by 18% compared to the global level. Hence, in this study, the
potential of palm kernel shell charcoal (PKSC) to decarbonize the rotary kiln-based SI production process was
analysed by framing three scenarios and comparing them with the business-as-usual (BAU). Meanwhile, the life
cycle assessment of the SI production through different scenarios was done to identify the sustainability of the
process. A cradle-to-gate approach was adopted, and it was found that during BAU, the net greenhouse gas (GHG)
emissions were 2525 CO2eq./t SI. However, usage of PKSC (scenario 3) in the SI production process aided in
achieving negative net GHG emissions of − 41 kg CO2eq./t SI. Meanwhile, the net GHG emission was 1092 kg
CO2eq./t SI and 1197 kg CO2eq./t when the coal used in the feed and injection end was replaced with PKSC in
scenario 1 and scenario 2, respectively. Thus, the usage of the PKSC instead of coal can abet in decarbonizing the
sponge iron industry thereby aiding in reducing the GHG emitted during the production of 1 t of steel in India to
2.4 t by 2030–31.

1. Introduction
The growing demand for iron and steel in diverse sectors has resulted
in a surge in global production, reaching a total of 1951 Mt in 2021
(World Steel in Figures, 2022). The energy-intensive iron and steel industry is responsible for 8% of global energy consumption (Iron and
Steel Technology Roadmap, 2020). As a result, the sector accounts for
3.7 GT of CO2 emissions due to its reliance on coal and coke to satisfy
approximately 74% of its energy demands (Malhotra et al., 2022). India
is the world’s second-largest producer of iron and steel, where the share
of coal and coke used to meet the sector’s energy demands has surged to
85% (Iron and Steel Technology Roadmap, 2020). Thus, leading to an
increase in energy consumption (18.8–20.9 GJ) and emissions (2.6 t
CO2eq.) happening during the production of 1 t of crude steel (tcs) by
30% and 40.5%, respectively compared to the global average (Government of India, 2022). Usage of steel with high carbon footprint to
construct solar (67.9 MW/t) and wind (107–132 MW/t) energy systems
reduces its decarbonization potential (Carrara et al., 2020). Meanwhile,
the transformation in electrical power sector (60% electricity from

renewable sources) is expected to increase the demand for steel, as wind
and solar photo-voltaic system of capacity 6 TW wind and 9 TW needs to
be installed (Bhaskar et al., 2021). Hence, to achieve global climate
goals by 2050, it is imperative to cut direct CO2 emissions (2.6 GT) by
50% (World Steel Association World Steel Association, 2022).
In this regard, the Indian government has set a target to reduce the
greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions of the iron and steel sector to 2.4 t
CO2eq./tcs by 2030 (Ministry of steel, 2017). The government also plans
to increase its production capacity from 113.6 Mt in 2021–22 to 300 Mt
by 2030, thereby underscoring the need for decarbonizing the iron and
steel industry (Ministry of Steel, 2022). Hence, to mitigate the carbon
footprint of the sector, recycled steel scrap was imported to increase its
proportion in total metallic inputs. However, the share of metallic scrap
contributes to 23% of total metallic inputs which is lower than the global
average of 32%. Hence, 25% of the iron manufactured in India was
sourced from direct reduction of iron (DRI) ore to produce SI (Elango,
2020). Thus, making them the frontrunner in SI production since 2003
(Association, 2020). During SI production, the iron ore is reduced below
its melting point to achieve 90–95% metallization that can fed in blast,

The SI can be produced by DRI ore in either rotary kiln or shaft energy by using coal, H2, and natural gas (NG) as energy sources (Ramakgala and Danha, 2019). Among these, the usage of H2 and NG for SI
production can aid in reducing its energy and carbon emissions. But the
share of green H2 is <1% of global H2 production. (Hydrogen). On the
other hand, shifting to NG-based SI production can lead to unsustainability over the years, with 46.3% of its demand being met through
imports (Ministry of coal and Natural gas, 2022). Moreover, the rotary
kiln process that is predominately employed for SI production in India is
incompatible to the use of NG and H2 for its operation. As a result, 79%
of the SI produced in India is derived from coal (World Steel Association,
2020). However, utilizing coal for SI production increases the CO2
emissions (3.4 t) by 2.4 times compared to NG usage.
In this context, it is necessary to identify a sustainable energy source
to substitute coal in the rotary kiln to decarbonize the sector. The fuel
used in rotary kiln must exhibit high reactivity and its fixed carbon (FC)
should be higher to ensure that the concentration of CO inside the kiln is
maintained around 50–60% to facilitate DRI ore (Dey et al., 2015). The
charcoal derived from biomass can satisfy the conditions specified above
(Dey et al., 2015).
Globally, the Indian economy holds the position of being the secondlargest agriculture-based economy and generates about 869.11 MT of
agricultural residues per year (Deka et al., 2023). Cereals, and sugarcane
account for approximately 77% of the agricultural residues generated in
India while oil seeds and pulses account for 10% and 9%, respectively
(Tagade et al., 2021). These residues are primarily generated during the
kharif and rabi seasons and are utilized as animal fodder, a source of
cooking energy in rural areas, and for thatching purposes (Kumar et al.,
2023). The surplus agricultural residues (288.14 MT/y) are generally
burnt in fields thereby leading to GHG emissions (1.4 t CO2/t of agricultural residue). However, the energy security of the nation can be
improved by converting these surplus agricultural residues into biofuels
by adopting advanced bioenergy techniques. Among the various bioenergy techniques, pyrolysis emerges as the promising option because of
its ability to produce biofuel at cheaper price (Ahmed et al., 2020).
Hence, the fuel characteristics of charcoal derived from various agricultural residues were analysed and juxtaposed with coal employed in
the rotary kiln-based SI production process (Han and Duan, 2015). The
analysis revealed that PKSC has the potential to replace coal in the rotary kiln.
Substituting coal with PKSC, a carbon-neutral fuel, in the rotary kiln
process can facilitate the decarbonization of SI production without the
need for any capital investment. Thus, enabling faster adoption by SI
industries. As most of the prior research on enhancement in efficiency of
the rotary kiln-based SI production necessitated capital investment due
to the integration of additional sub-system namely waste heat recovery
units (Kumar and Khanam, 2012), generators, steam turbines (Ramanaiah and Khanam, 2018), and gasifiers (Mohan Ghosh and N Vasudevan, 2021). These techniques were not embraced by the small-scale SI
industries that dominate Indian market. Charcoal derived from biomass
was used to decarbonize steel production in blast furnace/basic oxygen
furnace (Fan and Friedmann, 2021) and to manufacture SI in rotary
hearth furnace (Han and Duan, 2015). However, no work was carried
out to decarbonize the rotary kiln-based SI production without incurring
additional capital investment. At the same time, researchers are yet to
investigate the potential of utilizing charcoal derived from agricultural
waste as reducing agent and energy source for SI production.
Therefore, this study entails the substitution of coal with PKSC obtained from pyrolyzing palm kernel shells at 350 ◦ C to decarbonize the SI
production process without necessitating further investments. To
accomplish this, three distinct scenarios were developed by altering the
fuel input at both the feed and injection ends of the rotary kiln. Meanwhile, the system’s performance was assessed by comparing it to the
conventional business-as-usual (BAU) approach. The objective of this

study is to (i) Estimate the mass flow in different sub-systems; (ii) Assess
energy balance of SI production process for all the scenarios; (iii)
Determine the decarbonization potential and sustainability of SI production process by comparing the life cycle assessment of the three
scenarios with BAU.
2. Methodology
2.1. Business as usual
The conventional rotary kiln-based sponge iron (SI) production
process in India, which contributed to the manufacture of 33.88 million
tons of SI in 2022 was depicted in Fig. 1 (Ministry of Steel, 2023). The SI
production system of 100 ton per day (TPD) capacity comprised an inclined rotary kiln (3◦ ), a rotary cooler, an after burning chamber (ABC),
a waste heat recovery unit, an electrostatic precipitator (ESP), and a
magnetic separator. During the SI production process, lump ore, dolomite, and coal (non-coking) were fed into the rotary kiln through the
feeders. The fuel characteristics of feed and fuel coal are given in supplementary material S.Table 1 (Arabinda Sarangi, 2011).
At the discharge end of the rotary kiln, 6.2 t/h of fuel coal was
injected along with the primary air to produce SI. The heat generated
due to oxidation of the coal was utilized to elevate the temperature of
raw materials fed to the kiln to about 950–1025 ◦ C (Mohan Ghosh and N
Vasudevan, 2021). At this temperature, the iron ore (18.4 t/h) was
reduced to elemental Fe. The heat required for sustaining the endothermic reactions was also provided through the coal supplied at feed
end (6 t/h). Subsequently, the gases containing the unburnt component
of the feed and injection coal was directed to the ABC for complete
oxidation. Thus, ensuring that the concentration of CO in the flue gas
remained <1% (Sharma et al., 2019).
The flue gas from the ABC was passed through a waste heat recovery
(WHR) unit, where the heat energy present in the flue gas was utilized to
produce hot air. The hot air was employed to preheat the iron ore fed to
the kiln thereby improving the system efficiency. The flue gas exiting the
WHR unit was passed through an ESP to remove the dust particles suspended in it. Finally, the flue gases were released to the atmosphere via a
stack. On the injection end, the SI and char produced after reduction of
iron ore was discharged to the rotary cooler where it was cooled down to
100–120 ◦ C before being fed to the magnetic separator. In the magnetic
separator, the SI was separated from char with the aid of magnetic force.
2.1.1. Scenario 1
In this scenario, the fuel supplied at the injection end was PKSC at
5.8 t/h, in contrast to the coal (6.2 t/h) used in BAU. The reduction in
the quantity of fuel supplied can be attributed to the higher calorific
value of PKSC (6 Mcal) in comparison to the fuel coal (4.84 Mcal).
However, the total energy available in the injected fuel remained the
same irrespective of the scenarios adopted. The energy available in the
injected fuel must ensure that reduction environment was maintained at
the discharge thereby inhibiting reoxidation of SI. Meanwhile, the
quantity of metal oxide reduced to form SI and the C content in SI can be
regulated by the heat energy supplied at the discharge end (CPCB,
2007). It was assumed that about 67% of the C present in volatile matter
(VM) of the fuel introduced at the injection end underwent oxidation in
the kiln (Arabinda Sarangi, 2011).
2.1.2. Scenario 2
In this case, PKSC was introduced at the feed end instead of coal used
in BAU. Thus, PKSC served as a reducing agent in this scenario, in
contrast to its role as a heat source in scenario 1. Nevertheless, the
quantity of PKSC (4.6 t/h) fed to the kiln was determined by estimating
the FC present in the feed coal used in the BAU because the primary
function of the feed coal was to provide the necessary carbon for the
reduction of iron ore. The decrease in the quantity of PKSC can be
attributed to its higher FC concentration of 53.3% compared to feed coal

3. Result and discussion
LCA for rotary kiln-based SI production was carried out for three
different scenarios and compared with BAU. The mass and energy balance for 1 t of SI production using the rotary kiln system was computed
for all the scenarios. Input materials for all scenarios comprised of 1471
kg iron ore and 27 kg of dolomite. It was considered that the dolomite
was composed of 82.14% CaCO3 and only 40% of the CaO formed after
the decomposition of CaCO3 reacted with SO2 released during the
reduction process to produce CaSO3 (Yrjas et al., 1996). It was assumed
that the FC of the fuel (coal and PKSC) was completely oxidized in the
rotary kiln. 33% and 100% of VM present in the coal/PKSC supplied at
feed and injection ends, respectively were assumed to be oxidized at
ABC (Arabinda Sarangi, 2011).
3.1. Mass balance
The feed materials namely, coal, iron ore, and PKSC (excluding
dolomite) were crushed before it was fed to the rotary kiln. The air
required for the reduction process and the combustion of coal/PKSC was
determined based on the stochiometric condition (Carroll et al., 2013).
The quantity and composition of the ash, flue dust, and char generated
during the SI production in BAU were obtained from the literature
(Arabinda Sarangi, 2011). For the other scenarios, the composition of
ash and char (ash-69.6%, VM-1.9%, and FC-28.5%) was assumed to be
the same as that of BAU. In case of flue dust, the quantity of Fe2O3
present in the flue dust was assumed to be the same for all the scenarios
because the amount of iron ore fed to the rotary remained unchanged
irrespective of the scenarios. As the quantity of FC present in fuel supplied at the feed end was the same for all the scenarios. The quantity of
FC carried away with the flue dust was assumed to be constant for all the
scenarios. The quantity of VM present in the flue dust was assumed to be
contact (as the difference in composition VM of fuel employed at injection end is minimal) to simplify the mass and energy balance analysis.
However, the composition of the ash in fly dust varied based on the ash
content of the fuel (coal/PKSC) used in the respective scenarios. The
mass balance analysis for all scenarios is depicted in Fig. 4.
The quantity of fuel (PKSC - both feed and injection) consumed in
scenario 3 was found to be the least (832 kg) when compared to other
scenarios because of its high heating value (HHV) and FC content
compared to coal. Meanwhile, the ash content of PKSC was approximately 50% lower than coal used in rotary kiln for SI production. The
presence of 28.55% of O2 in PKSC aided in reducing the quantity of air
supplied to rotary kiln and ABC. Hence, the concentration of N2 in the
flue gas released during SI production through scenario 3 was the least
(58.15%) when compared to all the other scenarios.
On the other hand, during BAU, 976 kg of fuel was (coal – feed and
injection) consumed, the air supplied (5529 kg), and the flue gas
generated (6582 kg) was found to be highest when compared to other
scenarios. The increase in mass flow during BAU can be attributed to the
high ash and moisture content of coal. Meanwhile, the oxygen content of
coal (feed end-7.29% and fuel end-7.37%) used in BAU was approximately 3.9 times lower than the oxygen present in PKSC. Hence, the air
supplied to rotary kiln and ABC increases in case of BAU which in turn
led to increase in N2 concentration in the flue gas to 64.65% (highest
among all scenarios).
From the mass balance analysis of scenarios 1 and scenario 3, it was
identified that usage of PKSC at the injection end aided in reducing the
mass flow in the rotary kiln system when compared to its usage at the
feed end. As the replacement of 494 kg of coal containing 7.37% of O2
with 462 kg of PKSC containing 28.55% of O2 during scenario 1, aided in
reducing the quantity of air supplied from the atmosphere to the kiln.
Therefore, the total quantity of air required to produce 1 t of SI
decreased by 15.9%, 14.8%, and 35.6% for scenario 1, scenario 2, and
scenario 3, respectively, in comparison to the values reported in the

2.5. Sensitivity analysis
The sensitivity analysis explores the extremities indicated in literature and industry data. In this study, the influence of sulphur content in
both the feed and discharge coal on the greenhouse gas emissions during
SI production was assessed. The sulphur content was varied from 0.1 to
0.9% for different scenarios (excluding scenario 3 as PKSC does not
contain S). The presence of S concentration in coal beyond this range
affects the quality of SI (INDUSTRIAL TECHNICAL CONSULTANT).
Additionally, the effect of the FC to VM ratio was estimated by varying it
from 1.5 to 1.8 for all the scenarios, as suggested by the Bureau of Energy Efficiency of India (Sharma et al., 2019).

3.5. Fuel selection
Decarbonization of the sponge iron industry can be achieved by
substituting the coal used in SI production (BAU) with a sustainable
energy source. The chosen fuel for the rotary kiln should exhibit high
reactivity, and its FC content must be higher to ensure that the concentration of CO inside the kiln is maintained around 50–60% to facilitate the direct reduction of iron ore (Tagade et al., 2021). The charcoal
derived from biomass can satisfy the conditions specified above (Ahmed
et al., 2020). Hence, the fuel characteristics of charcoal derived from
various agricultural wastes were analysed and compared with coal utilized in the rotary kiln-based SI production process (Arabinda Sarangi,
2011). Charcoal derived from fuel wood was excluded from consideration due to concerns regarding its sustainability. Meanwhile, PKSC was
employed as reducing agent instead of coal to produce Fe and MnSiO4
from FeTiO3 (Setiawan et al., 2020) and MnO2 (Suharto et al., 2020),
respectively. Thus, affirming its potential as a reducing agent.
An analysis of the fuel characteristics of PKSC revealed its potential
to replace coal in rotary kilns. The FC to the VM ratio of PKSC was 1.5
which falls within the range (1.5–1.8) suggested by the Bureau of Energy
Efficiency of India (illustrated in Fig. 3) (Nduagu et al., 2022). In
addition, the bio-oil and syngas generated during the pyrolysis of palm
kernel shell at 350 ◦ C can satisfy the energy required during the SI
production process. Thus, making PKSC, a carbon-neutral energy source.
The absence of sulphur and moisture content in the PKSC added to its
advantage (Kumar and Khanam, 2012). Furthermore, the higher calorific value (24.99 MJ/kg) and lower ash content (11.7%) of PKSC aided
in reducing the cost and emissions incurred during transportation when
compared to coal.
Approximately 600 million tonnes (MT) of palm kernel shell are
produced annually in India, capable of producing 276 MT of PKSC.
Utilization of PKSC instead of coal in rotary kiln for SI production coal
has the potential to replace 313 t of coal/a that can be used to produce
321 t of SI. Thus, mitigating the emission of 810.5 t CO2eq./a (Ministry of
Road Transport and Highways, 2023).

3.7. Sensitivity analysis
When coal, PKSC, iron ore, dolomite, and electricity are sourced
from the same location, the variability in SI production using rotary kiln
process can be attributed to the composition of fuel used during its
production. Among them, S content and the ratio of FC to VM are the
main parameters influencing SI production. However, for effective
production of SI using rotary kiln process, the S content in the fuel
should not be >0.9% and the ratio of FC to VM should be between 1.5
and 1.8. Therefore, the impact of these parameters on the GHGs emitted
during the SI production process (excluding supply chain and electricity)
was evaluated for all the scenarios adopted in this study.

when the S content in the fuel was raised from 0.1 to 0.9%.

(Fig. 10). However, in the BAU, GHG emissions decreased from 1906 kg

4. Limitations and challenges
Replacement of coal with PKSC in the rotary kiln can abet in
decarbonizing the SI industry thereby aiding in reducing the GHG
emitted during the production of 1 t of steel in India to 2.4 t by 2030–31
(Government of India, 2022). The impact on land usage change, water
usage, food security, and other environmental parameters must be
assessed before expanding the cultivation area of palm trees to harvest
palm kernel shells (Bellamy et al., 2021). However, ensuring a continuous year-round supply of feedstock poses challenges.
Palm kernel shells are widely utilized as low-cost feedstock to
manufacture activated carbon which are used as bio absorbents for gas/
liquid separation and wastewater treatment (Uchegbulam et al., 2022).
Meanwhile, it is employed as reducing agent in pyrometallurgical process, aggregates for the construction of building and feedstocks to produce advanced biofuel (Rashidi and Yusup, 2017). However, most of the
palm kernel shells are either incinerated or discarded into atmosphere
without extracting the energy present in it. Thus, causing negative
impact on economics and environment. Hence, implementing effective
mechanism to collect the palm kernel shell without expanding its
cultivation area will aid in lowering the above-mentioned environmental impacts, as it is a carbon neutral energy source.
Despite being carbon neutral, the cost of charcoal derived from
biomass varies from $295/t to $525/t which is higher than the cost of
coal used in rotary kiln (~$162.6/t) (Suopajä et al., 2013). However,
implementation of a uniform carbon tax across nations and policies
mandating the usage biomass-based energy sources to produce SI will
facilitate in achieving decarbonization of the sector at a faster rate
(Ganeshan et al., 2023). Presently, the carbon tax levied in various
countries ranges from $47.1/t CO2 eq. to $198.7/t CO2eq. (Mousa et al.,
2016).

5. Conclusion
The iron and steel industry contributes to 7% of global GHG emissions, necessitating decarbonization to meet climate targets. India, the
second largest steel producer, relies on coal to satisfy 85% of the sector’s
energy demand, emphasizing the need to find a sustainable alternative.
Hence, the potential of PKSC to decarbonize the SI industry was
explored in this study.
Switching from coal to PKSC not only resulted in negative net GHG
emissions (− 41 kg CO2eq./t SI) but also aided in attaining a sustainable
circular economy. Negative emission was achieved in scenario 3 as PKSC
usage resulted in the avoidance of 976 kg of coal thereby mitigating the
emission (304.7 kg CO2eq.) happening in the upstream of coal mining
process. Thus, scenario 3 achieved negative emissions, even though
there was an emission of 263.8 kg CO2eq. in the upstream related to
production of electricity, iron ore, and dolomite. Even the partial
replacement of coal with PKSC (scenario 1 and scenario 2) at the feed/
injection end reduced the SI production process’s carbon footprint by
~55%. However, ensuring a continuous year-round supply of feedstock
poses challenges. Hence, identifying an energy source by blending
charcoal from coconut or cashew shells with char from agricultural

scenarios. The GHG emissions during the production of 1 ton of SI
decreased by 3.6%, 8.3%, and 8.2% for BAU, scenario 1, and scenario 2,
respectively, when 100% of the electricity was sourced from renewable
energy. On the other hand, GHG emissions decreased by approximately
1.8 times, 2.5 times, 3.3 times, and 4 times when 25%, 50%, 75%, and
100% of the electricity used for SI production was derived from
renewable energy sources.
